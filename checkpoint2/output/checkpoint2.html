0. What is compared and how¶

Three model classes are built on set-a with the same cross-validation (CV) folds: a LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) logistic regression, a logistic generalised additive model (GAM) with cubic splines, and a gradient-boosted tree ensemble. Two published severity scores, SAPS-I (Simplified Acute Physiology Score I) and SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment), are evaluated on the same folds as benchmarks. Acronyms are defined at first use.

The rest of the report follows the checklist: the modelling table (section 1), predictors (section 2), the CV design (section 3), the three models (sections 4 to 6), the comparison and the final model (section 7), the saved artefacts (section 8) and limitations (section 9).

1. Data, outcome and layout¶

Table 1 shows the size of the modelling table used everywhere below: 4,000 ICU (intensive care unit) stays with 554 in-hospital deaths (13.85%). That is the whole cohort, not a subset, and no stay is lost when the predictors are built. The outcome is In-hospital_death from Outcomes-a.txt, joined to the measurements by RecordID. The layout is one row per ICU stay: all time-series predictors summarise hours 0 to 48 of that stay, so a stay contributes exactly one row and each row is in exactly one CV fold. Because a stay cannot appear in two folds, no grouped CV is needed here; the code checks that RecordID is unique, that there are 4,000 rows, and that the outcome is complete.

The cleaning decisions from Checkpoint 1 are carried over:

  • The value -1 is the challenge's code for "not measured" and was set to missing (2,247 raw values). The same code in SAPS-I and SOFA was set to missing (190 and 144 stays); those stays are skipped only when the benchmark scores are evaluated.
  • FiO2 was recorded in percent for part of the cohort: values between 1 and 100 were divided by 100, and FiO2 = 0 was treated as missing (inspired oxygen fraction below 0.21 is impossible).
  • Height recorded in metres (1.2 to 2.5) or inches (40 to 90) was converted to centimetres; values outside 100 to 250 cm were dropped, as were weight outside 20 to 300 kg and age outside 15 to 120 years.
  • Physiologically implausible values were dropped using the variable-specific bounds from Checkpoint 1 (for example heart rate 20 to 250 bpm, temperature 30 to 45 degrees C, pH 6.5 to 8.0, creatinine 0.1 to 30 mg/dL).
  • All records fall inside 0 to 48 hours, so no observation is excluded by time.
  • Admission weight is the value recorded at 00:00. Later weight measurements were not used because they largely reflect fluid resuscitation during the window.

Read Table 4 by group. Admission variables are age, sex, height, admission weight and the three ICU-type dummies (medical ICU, MICU, is the reference). Latest value, minimum, maximum and trend describe each of 24 time-series variables: the most recent value, the worst low, the worst high, and the least-squares slope per hour (slope is computed for the eight variables whose direction of change is clinically central: heart rate, systolic pressure, temperature, GCS, BUN, creatinine, lactate and white cell count). Monitoring intensity is the number of valid measurements of each variable (0 when never measured), total urine volume over 48 hours, and whether the patient was ever recorded as mechanically ventilated. Missingness holds the never-measured indicators for the variables in Table 2 and indicators for admission values that were not recorded (height, weight, sex, ICU type).

Three choices need a reason:

  • ICUType is categorical, not a number from 1 to 4; the three dummies are used with MICU (medical intensive care unit) as the reference, so the coefficients are contrasts against MICU. The others are CSRU (cardiac surgery recovery unit), CCU (coronary care unit) and SICU (surgical intensive care unit).
  • Length_of_stay and Survival are never predictors. They describe what happened after the 48-hour window and would be leakage; they are not even read into the modelling table.
  • SAPS-I and SOFA are benchmarks, not predictors. They are kept in the table for section 7 only and are excluded from the predictor list in code.

The count of measurements doubles as a missingness predictor: a variable that was measured 30 times tells us the patient was monitored closely, and a variable never measured tells us the team did not suspect that problem. These are clinical decisions, not random missingness, so they are kept as predictors but will not be read causally (see section 9).

3. Cross-validation design¶

The design is stratified 5-fold CV on set-a, with the folds split by stay (here one row per stay, so each stay is in exactly one fold and no grouped CV is required). The seed is fixed at 707, and the fold vector is saved to output/cp2_folds.csv. Every model, including the two benchmarks, uses exactly these folds, so a difference between two models is not a difference between two fold splits.

Two leakage controls matter:

  1. Preprocessing is fitted inside the training fold. Median imputation and standardisation are fitted on the 3,200 training rows and then applied to the 800 held-out rows, through a scikit-learn pipeline (LASSO) or an explicit imputer inside the GAM class. Imputing or scaling all 4,000 stays before splitting would let the held-out fold influence the training data.
  2. Hyperparameters are tuned with an inner CV on the training rows only. For LASSO and the GAM the inner loop is 4-fold; for the gradient-boosting model it is 3-fold, because each boosting fit takes a few seconds and the grid is small. The inner loop minimises log loss, which rewards predicted risks close to the outcome rather than ranking alone; AUROC is then reported as the primary discrimination measure.

The winning configuration's CV score is still optimistic: the same set-a is used to rank configurations and to pick the winner. That is why set-b and set-c are held back for a single-look estimate in Checkpoint 3. Table 5 shows the folds are balanced (800 stays and 110 to 111 deaths each).

4. Model 1: regularised logistic regression (LASSO)¶

I used an L1 (LASSO) penalty. There are 124 candidate predictors, many of them strongly correlated (BUN with creatinine, invasive with non-invasive blood pressure, several summaries of the same variable), and 554 events. L1 shrinks and selects at the same time, so it produces a sparse scorecard that a clinician could read; ridge would keep all 124 coefficients, and elastic net adds a second tuning parameter that this event count does not support. Predictors are standardised inside the fold, so the penalty is applied on a common scale and the coefficients plotted in Figure 1 are log-odds per 1 standard deviation.

The penalty strength C was chosen by inner 4-fold CV on the training rows, over nine values from 1e-4 to 1. The inner search chose C = 0.1 in every outer fold (Table 6), which is a moderately strong penalty: 67 of the 124 predictors are retained in the refit on all of set-a.

Table 6 shows the per-fold AUROC required by the checklist: mean 0.833 with SD 0.020, and the fold-to-fold spread (0.805 to 0.863) is larger than the difference between the three models. Table 7 shows why C = 0.1 was chosen. The minimum is interior, not at the edge of the grid: mean inner log loss is 0.305 at C = 0.1, 0.312 at 0.32 and 0.315 at 0.03, and it degrades at both ends (0.322 at C = 1, 0.355 at C = 0.01).

Figure 1 plots the coefficients of the model refit on all of set-a, on the standardised scale, so bar lengths are comparable, and Table 8 lists the fifteen largest in numbers. The strongest signals are the last GCS (lower is worse), age, whether respiratory rate was ever measured, CSRU versus MICU, the minimum BUN and the minimum temperature (lower temperature, i.e. hypothermia, is worse). This is clinically sensible: level of consciousness and age dominate, renal function and hypothermia add risk, and the CSRU cohort (a cardiac-surgery recovery unit) is at lower risk than MICU.

5. Model 2: non-linear effects with a logistic GAM¶

Model 2 is a logistic GAM with cubic-spline terms (5 knots) for 14 continuous predictors and linear terms for the ICU-type dummies, mechanical ventilation and two missingness indicators, 20 predictors in total. They were chosen from the stable Model-1 predictors, keeping one summary per clinical domain so that every smooth is estimable and the partial-effect plots are readable: Age, GCS_last, HR_max, SysABP_min, Temp_min, pH_min, PaO2_min, BUN_last, Creatinine_max, Na_min, WBC_max, Platelets_min, Lactate_max, Urine_sum (splines) and ICUType_CCU, ICUType_CSRU, ICUType_SICU, MechVent_any, RespRate_never, LiverPanel_never (linear).

Two consequences are stated openly. First, Model 2 has 104 fewer predictors than Model 1, so part of any performance difference reflects that, not only the spline form. Second, this is the model the checklist asks for: a manageable set of predictors with flexible shape.

The smoothing strength was chosen by inner 4-fold CV over five values (0.6 to 30). I did not include smaller values because pygam's fitting algorithm did not converge for them on these data; lam = 0.6 was chosen in every fold, the least smoothing in the grid.

Figure 2 shows the partial effects on the log-odds scale, centred at the median of each predictor, with 95% confidence bands; Table 12 converts the same curves to odds ratios (OR) relative to the median. What each curve shows, and whether it makes clinical sense:

  • Age rises monotonically (OR 1.77, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.84, at 85 versus 67 years; OR 0.51, 0.34 to 0.77, at 40). The curve is close to linear, so the spline adds little here but confirms that no threshold effect is being missed.
  • GCS_last is clearly non-linear and is the strongest predictor: at a GCS of 7 the OR is 4.40 (3.09 to 6.25) against 15, and above about 13 the curve is flat. A steep rise only at low coma scores is clinically expected, because GCS is bounded at 15 and most patients are near the ceiling; this is exactly the shape a linear term cannot represent.
  • Temp_min is monotone (OR 1.33, 0.72 to 2.44, at 35.0 degrees C versus 36.0): hypothermia is associated with higher risk. The confidence band is wide and includes 1, so this is suggestive, not established. I expected a U-shape, but the high-temperature side is not visible in the minimum temperature by construction; the maximum is in the model's predictor pool but not in the GAM.
  • BUN_last rises with urea (OR 2.47, 1.24 to 4.93, at 51 versus 19 mg/dL), monotone, consistent with renal failure and with the positive BUN_min coefficient in Model 1.
  • Lactate_max is nearly flat (OR 1.12, 0.55 to 2.27, at 5.8 versus 2.3 mmol/L). This is surprising given the literature, and I read it as a limitation of the data rather than evidence that lactate is unimportant: 45% of stays have no lactate, the median-imputed values pull the curve toward the middle, and lactate is strongly correlated with BUN and creatinine, which are already in the model.
  • SysABP_min is also flat (OR 1.03, 0.58 to 1.84). After age, GCS, BUN and lactate are accounted for, the lowest systolic pressure in the first 48 hours adds little; vasopressor dose, which would carry this information, is not in the challenge data.

Two of the six curves are effectively flat (lactate and the lowest systolic pressure), four are monotone in a clinically sensible direction (age, GCS, temperature and BUN), and only GCS shows curvature that a linear term would clearly miss. That is a useful negative result, and it is consistent with the small AUROC difference between Models 1 and 2.

6. Model 3: tree ensemble (gradient boosting)¶

Model 3 is scikit-learn's histogram gradient boosting classifier on all 124 predictors. The tuning grid is small and stated here: learning rate in {0.05, 0.10}, maximum number of leaves in {7, 15, 31}, minimum number of samples per leaf fixed at 20; at most 300 trees, with early stopping after 20 iterations without improvement and an internal validation set of 15% of the training fold. The stopping set therefore never contains held-out patients. Missing values are passed to the model natively (no imputation): each split sends a missing value to whichever side reduced the loss in training, which is appropriate here because missingness is informative rather than random. The configuration was chosen by inner 3-fold CV on log loss.

Figure 3 overlays the out-of-fold receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves of all five candidates, and Figure 4 shows the fold-to-fold variation. Both tell the same story: the three models are bunched together, well above the two scores.

Does the best model beat SAPS-I and SOFA by more than the fold-to-fold variation? Yes, by a wide margin. Gradient boosting reaches 0.851 against 0.649 for SAPS-I and 0.623 for SOFA, a difference of about 0.20, roughly five times the SD of SAPS-I across folds (0.038) and far more than the SD of SOFA (0.012). The model class, not lucky folds, is responsible. The same holds for LASSO and the GAM, which are each about 0.18 above SAPS-I.

Is the best model better than the other two? Gradient boosting has the highest mean AUROC (0.851 versus 0.833 and 0.832), the lowest out-of-fold log loss (0.299 versus 0.309) and the lowest Brier score (0.092 versus 0.094), and it wins in all five folds against LASSO, by 0.002 to 0.029 (mean 0.018, about 0.7 of the LASSO fold SD). LASSO and the GAM are statistically indistinguishable from each other. So the ranking of Model 3 above Models 1 and 2 is consistent but not dramatic; the large, unambiguous gap is between any of the models and the published scores. This is itself a finding worth reporting: most of the gain comes from using the full 48-hour multivariate record rather than a single severity score, not from the last increment of model complexity.

Final model. I chose Model 3, the gradient-boosted tree ensemble, because it had the best discrimination in every fold, the best log loss and the best Brier score. The price is interpretability: its decision function is not a scorecard. I accept that price because Checkpoint 3 requires a calibrated risk and an explanation of the drivers, which permutation importance and partial dependence can supply, and because the comparison is about prediction, not about mechanism. A clinician-facing scorecard would be Model 1 at a small cost in AUROC; I report that as the alternative.

The final model is refitted on all 4,000 stays of set-a, with the configuration re-selected by 5-fold CV on set-a, and saved together with its fitted preprocessing (the GBM needs none; the feature order is stored with it).

9. Limitations and reproducibility¶

  • Single source of data. set-a is one cohort from the 2012 challenge; nothing here is external validation. The CV estimate is also optimistic because the same data rank the configurations and choose the winner. set-b and set-c are the single-look test in Checkpoint 3.
  • The 48-hour window and what it omits. Treatments are not recorded: vasopressor dose, sedation, fluid balance and goals-of-care decisions are missing. GCS is measured under sedation in ventilated patients, which biases the strongest predictor toward the sedation policy of each unit. Lactate is missing in 45% of stays and height in 48%; median imputation within folds handles the mechanics but not the possibility that values are missing not at random, and the partial-effect curves for lactate and BUN are conditional on that imputation.
  • Missingness as a predictor. Never-measured indicators and measurement counts improve prediction, but they encode monitoring intensity and local practice. A model that uses them may travel poorly to a unit with different test-ordering habits, and they must not be read causally.
  • Benchmarks. SAPS-I and SOFA are unavailable for 190 and 144 stays; the benchmark AUROCs are computed on the remaining stays in each fold, which slightly changes the effective test set for those two rows.
  • Tuning effort. The boosting grid has six configurations and a 3-fold inner loop to keep the runtime of a few minutes; 7 leaves is at the edge of the grid, so a larger search might gain a little. The LASSO and GAM grids are coarse in the same spirit.
  • What AUROC does not measure. AUROC ignores calibration, and a model can rank well and still give wrong absolute risks. Calibration, decision curves, subgroups, interpretation and error analysis are deliberately left to Checkpoint 3.
  • Reproducibility. Seeds are fixed (cp2_lib.SEED = 707, also passed to every fold's tuning), the folds are saved, all paths are relative to this folder, and pixi.lock pins the packages, so pixi run --locked checkpoint2 reproduces the numbers in this report.
