0. What is evaluated and how¶

The task is to predict in-hospital death from the first 48 hours of an intensive care unit (ICU) stay. Checkpoint 2 is abbreviated CP2 in table headers. The report follows the checklist. Section 1 applies the model to set-b and set-c. Section 2 covers discrimination: the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CI), the comparison with SAPS-I (Simplified Acute Physiology Score I) and SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment), the comparison with the cross-validation (CV) estimate, and the precision-recall (PR) curve. Section 3 covers calibration: a flexible loess calibration curve, the calibration intercept and slope, and the Brier score. Section 4 covers clinical usefulness: decision curve analysis (DCA), a proposed threshold, and the 2012 challenge Event 1 score. Section 5 presents the subgroups, section 6 the interpretation (SHAP, SHapley Additive exPlanations, and partial dependence against the Checkpoint 2 GAM, generalised additive model), section 7 the most confident errors, and section 8 the conclusions.

set-b plays the role of the primary test set and set-c serves as a second, independent test set. The 2012 challenge kept set-c hidden and used it only for its final scores.

3. Calibration (3.3)¶

Calibration is assessed by the method recommended in Evaluating clinical prediction models, part 1: a flexible calibration curve plus the calibration intercept and slope. I do not rely on the Hosmer-Lemeshow test.

  • Flexible curve. A loess smoother (statsmodels lowess, span 0.4, no robustness iterations, which would otherwise treat the rare deaths as outliers) estimates observed mortality as a function of predicted risk. The 95% band comes from 200 bootstrap resamples. The histogram under each curve shows how many stays lie at each predicted risk, on a log scale.
  • Calibration intercept (calibration-in-the-large): a logistic regression of the outcome on an intercept only, with logit(predicted risk) as an offset. The ideal value is 0. A value above 0 means the model under-predicts on average.
  • Calibration slope: the coefficient from a logistic regression of the outcome on logit(predicted risk). The ideal value is 1. Below 1, predictions are too extreme (overfitting); above 1, they are too conservative.
  • Brier score: the mean squared difference between predicted risk and outcome. The scaled Brier is 1 − Brier / Brier of a model that predicts the mortality rate for everyone, so 0 means no better than that model and 1 means perfect.

4. Clinical usefulness (3.4)¶

Proposed use. At the end of the first 48 hours in the ICU, the model would flag every patient whose predicted risk of in-hospital death is at least 0.30. The flag would trigger a structured goals-of-care conversation with the patient or family, led by the attending physician, within the next 24 hours, with a palliative-care consult offered. The flag does not withdraw or ration treatment. It makes sure that an important conversation is not left until a crisis.

Why 0.30. A threshold probability p states how many false alarms one accepts per true case: at 0.30, the odds are 0.30/0.70, so about 2.3 conversations with patients who survive are judged acceptable for each conversation with a patient who dies. A goals-of-care conversation has little potential for harm, but it takes senior clinician time and can distress families. The threshold should therefore be well above the 14% base rate, but not so high that most dying patients are missed. The threshold is set by a rule that uses set-a only, so set-b and set-c play no part in choosing it. Table 6 uses Checkpoint 2's out-of-fold predictions, and 0.30 is the round number closest to the threshold that maximises the challenge's Event 1 score, min(Se, PPV), on set-a (0.29). Se is the sensitivity and PPV the positive predictive value, the share of flagged patients who die.

Which differences are large enough to matter? Table 8 makes ten comparisons, and several subgroups are small (CSRU has only 34 deaths, under-50s 68), so any single difference should be judged against its interval and against the number of comparisons.

  • Age. The AUROC falls from about 0.90 under 50 to about 0.83 at 65 and over, and the intervals for the youngest and the two oldest groups only just touch. This is the one pattern I consider real. It is also expected: age is itself a strong predictor, and within an older band the patients are more alike in the rest of their physiology, so ranking them is harder. Mean predicted risk follows observed mortality closely in every age group (all four intercept intervals contain 0, the 65 to 79 group's only just).
  • ICU type. The AUROC ranges from about 0.81 in the MICU (medical ICU) to about 0.88 in the SICU (surgical ICU). The MICU interval (0.78 to 0.84) lies just below the SICU interval (0.86 to 0.91) and does not overlap it. Medical patients are a mixed group (sepsis, respiratory failure, cancer), and their deaths depend more on the underlying disease, which the 48-hour physiology captures less well. The CSRU (cardiac surgery recovery unit) AUROC rests on 34 deaths, and its very wide interval (about 0.79 to 0.94) allows no conclusion. All four ICU intercept intervals contain 0.
  • Sex. The AUROCs for women and men (0.85 and 0.87) have overlapping intervals. Men are slightly under-predicted (intercept +0.16, interval just above 0; observed 13.7% against a predicted 12.4%). It is the only one of the ten intercept intervals that excludes zero, and about 0.5 such intervals are expected by chance alone, so I report it as a pattern to monitor, not as evidence of a sex-specific miscalibration.

In short, the model works in every subgroup, with an AUROC of at least 0.81 everywhere and no clinically important miscalibration. Ranking is weaker among older and medical-ICU patients, and that is where the threshold-based flag will miss the largest share of deaths.

Overall, the two model classes agree on the direction of all three effects and on the shape of the dominant one (GCS). Where they disagree (very high BUN), the reason is how the tree ensemble shares the effect among correlated predictors, not a contradictory finding.

Why the important predictors must not be read causally. The model predicts death from what was recorded in the first 48 hours. Many records reflect the care given and the decisions made, not only the patient's biology. Examples from this model:

  • MechVent_n is the fourth most important predictor by SHAP. In the low-risk deaths in section 7, a small count (4 to 6 ventilation records) pushes the risk down. Those are mostly fast-track cardiac surgery patients who were taken off the ventilator early. The model has learned that being taken off the ventilator early is a marker of a good course. It would be absurd to conclude that ventilating fewer patients would save lives.
  • RespRate_never raises predicted risk (LASSO coefficient +0.25 in Checkpoint 2, and in the permutation top five here). Respiratory rate is missing from the records of most ventilated patients, so the indicator mostly flags ventilation and unit documentation habits. Recording a respiratory rate cannot lower anyone's risk.
  • Urine_sum is the total of the urine volumes recorded. It depends on the number of hours with a recorded volume (catheter in place, nurse charting) as well as on kidney function. A low total partly means "few recorded hours".
  • GCS_last is measured under sedation in ventilated patients, so a low score partly reflects the sedation policy. Lightening sedation to raise the number would not change the prognosis.

These predictors are useful for prediction, because they are recorded by hour 48 in the same way in set-b and set-c, but they carry no causal effect. A causal question (for example "does early extubation reduce mortality?") would need a design that deals with confounding by indication, which this model does not.

What the ten errors have in common.

  • High-risk survivors (predicted risk about 0.79 to 0.86). Three of the five (148302, 147053, 150629) were genuinely, severely ill, with a low GCS, shock, acidosis and SOFA scores of 14 to 15. The model's estimate is reasonable and these patients were the survivors that an 80% risk allows for. The error lies in information not in the data, such as the response to treatment after hour 48. The other two show data problems. Stay 150779 has 38 missing predictors, a urine total built from only 15 charted hours, and temperatures of 33 to 34 °C in an awake patient. Stay 148066 has a GCS of 7 that most likely reflects sedation in a ventilated 90-year-old with otherwise reassuring values.
  • Low-risk deaths (predicted risk 0.005 to 0.01). All five were post-operative patients (four CSRU, one SICU) with the classic fast-track pattern: early extubation (only 4 to 6 ventilation records), GCS back to 15 after anaesthesia, and generous urine output. For three of them, nothing in hours 0 to 48 announces the death, which must have followed a later complication. That information is absent from the data, and no 48-hour model could have caught it. For two (146206 and, in part, 151006) there were warning signs (systolic pressure down to 50 mmHg, pH 7.19, a WBC of 25.8, SOFA 12) that the model under-weighted, because the fast-track predictors pushed the risk down so strongly. That is a model error. A tree ensemble learns that CSRU plus early extubation is almost always safe and gives too little weight to the rare exceptions.

The practical lesson is that the confident misses come from a single, recognisable pattern (post-operative fast-track patients). A deployed tool should not replace clinical review for patients with abnormal haemodynamics or lactate, however low their predicted risk.

8. Conclusions (3.8)¶

Clinical usefulness: would I recommend the model for the use proposed in section 4? I would recommend it for a supervised pilot as a prompt for goals-of-care conversations at hour 48. I would not recommend it as an unsupervised decision rule. The evidence for the pilot:

  • It discriminates well and consistently on two test sets it never saw (AUROC 0.86 on both, about 0.20 above SAPS-I and SOFA), and the CV estimate from Checkpoint 2 was not optimistic.
  • It is well calibrated (slope about 1; mean prediction about one percentage point below observed mortality), so a predicted 30% can be read as roughly a 30% risk.
  • At the pre-specified threshold of 0.30 it flags about 14 patients per 100, about half of whom die in hospital, and catches about half of all deaths. It has a higher net benefit than SOFA, SAPS-I, treating everyone or treating no one across the relevant range of thresholds.

The reservations: the flag misses about half of the deaths (Table 7), notably in post-operative fast-track patients who deteriorate later (section 7); ranking is weaker in older and medical-ICU patients (section 5); and the recorded inputs depend on local documentation and treatment practice. The conversation the flag triggers carries little risk of harm. That is why the case for a pilot is reasonable, provided clinicians keep the final say and the model is recalibrated at least in its intercept and audited before it is used at another hospital.

Limitations.

  • Single data source. set-a, set-b and set-c are random thirds of one challenge database from the same hospitals and period. set-b and set-c are therefore internal, temporally concurrent validation, not external validation. Performance at a different hospital, or years later with different practice, is untested.
  • The 48-hour window. Predictions are made at hour 48 for patients who stayed that long. Deaths caused by events after hour 48 (section 7) are not predictable from these data, and the model says nothing about earlier decision points.
  • Missing data. Many predictors are often missing (height in about half of the stays, lactate in 45%). The model uses missingness as information, which helps when testing habits are the same but makes the model fragile when they change. Some records are sparse or implausible (stay 150779).
  • No treatment information. Vasopressor doses, sedation, fluid balance and do-not-resuscitate or comfort-care decisions are absent. They confound several predictors (GCS, ventilation, urine) and limit what the model can explain.
  • Benchmarks. SAPS-I and SOFA are missing for about 4% of stays, and their AUROCs are computed on the rest. For the DCA they were turned into probabilities with a logistic map fitted on set-a.
  • Statistical uncertainty. The subgroup and decision-curve results rest on 568 set-b deaths; small subgroups (CSRU, under-50s) have wide intervals.

Leakage risks and how the design prevents them.

  • Post-window outcomes as predictors. Length_of_stay and Survival describe what happened after hour 48. They never enter the modelling table, and the Checkpoint 2 code asserts this.
  • Benchmarks as predictors. SAPS-I and SOFA are kept out of the predictor list and used only as comparators.
  • Preprocessing and tuning leakage on set-a. In Checkpoint 2, imputation and scaling (LASSO, GAM) were fitted inside each training fold, hyperparameters were tuned by inner CV, and the GBM's early-stopping set came from the training fold.
  • Test-set leakage. set-b and set-c were never used to fit, tune or choose anything. The model is loaded from a file (with its SHA-256 printed), the IDs are checked to be disjoint, the threshold was chosen on set-a out-of-fold predictions, and the SOFA and SAPS-I maps were fitted on set-a. The one model fitted in this notebook, the comparison GAM in section 6, was fitted on set-a only.
  • Soft leakage within the window. A decision made during hours 0 to 48 to move to comfort care would reduce monitoring (fewer measurements, tests not ordered) and also predict death. The count and never-measured predictors could therefore partly encode clinicians' prognoses rather than physiology. The design cannot rule this out, because the data do not record such decisions. It is a reason to read the model's high-risk flags as a prompt for a conversation, not as an independent verdict.

Could the predictors be mistaken for causes? Yes, easily, which is why section 6 warns against it. The model's most important inputs mix physiology with care processes: a low sedated GCS, early extubation (MechVent_n), charted urine volume, and whether a respiratory rate was recorded. None of these is a lever. Intervening on them (sedating less, extubating earlier, charting more often) would not change a patient's risk in the way the SHAP values suggest. The model answers "who is at high risk?", not "what would lower the risk?".
